01The thesis in one paragraph

Buy control of regional B2B companies that are fundamentally sound but under-managed: profitable or near break-even, growing at 30–40% (too slow for VC, too good to ignore). Rather than a passive minority stake, use a hybrid PE / venture-building model — install talent, fix finance and cap table, restructure costs — to force a step-change in value. "Rule of 40" is a loose screen (growth % + margin % ≈ 40), not a hard criterion.

02Sub-strategy A — SaaS secondary buyout & turnaround

Why it can work

  • Forced-seller dynamics. Many 2020–22 vintage VC-backed SaaS companies raised at valuations the market no longer supports. Their investors need distributions or write-offs, and founders are tired. A credible, fast, certain offer has real value.
  • Founder-first sourcing sidesteps competitive VC-syndicate processes and gets the founder aligned before the cap table is engaged.
  • Fixable gaps are cheap to fix (CFO, pricing, sales discipline) relative to the value unlocked.

Where it can break

  • Adverse selection. Why is this company available at a discount? Often because the growth is not durable. Mitigate with cohort-level diligence, not headline ARR.
  • Preference stacks and drag/tag rights. Even "less rigid" stacks can block a deal if one investor holds out. Map every holder's incentives (some prefer a write-off to a discounted sale for fund-accounting reasons).
  • Founder retention after liquidity. A founder who takes secondary and is fatigued may mentally leave. Structure with earn-outs, rollover equity, and a named successor/CEO plan.
  • Valuation anchoring. "Discount to last round" is a negotiating frame, not a valuation method. Anchor on revenue/ARR multiples, gross-margin-adjusted, and on a standalone cash-flow view.

Deal structure ideas

  • Secondary for founders + early investors, primary for the turnaround budget (CFO, hires, restructuring) — keep the two clearly separated.
  • Rollover for founder (e.g. 20–40%) to keep alignment.
  • Earn-out or deferred consideration tied to NRR/ARR milestones to bridge valuation gaps.
  • Consider structured instruments (preferred with a liquidation preference) if buying less than 100%.

The playbook as a 100-day plan

Window Focus
Days 0–30 Finance baseline: ARR bridge, cohorts, cash runway, covenant/contract review; appoint interim CFO
Days 30–60 Org: confirm key-person risk, begin senior searches, cap-table cleanup (option pool, dead equity)
Days 60–100 Commercial: pricing/packaging review, sales-process reset, cost plan, KPI cadence (board pack monthly)

03Sub-strategy B — Platform / consolidation ("Constellation")

The logic

Combine turned-around A-companies and bolt-ons into a shared platform. Value comes from (1) multiple arbitrage — small assets bought at low multiples, sold/valued as a larger platform at higher multiples; (2) shared cost base — finance, hosting, support, security, procurement; (3) cross-sell where customer bases overlap.

Honest reading of the economics

This is closer to permanent-capital serial acquirer (the Constellation Software model) than to a venture fund. That has consequences:

Dimension Venture fund Roll-up / holdco
Hold period 3–7 yrs 10+ yrs, often indefinite
Return source Exit multiple on growth Reinvested free cash flow + multiple arbitrage
Fee logic Fee on committed/invested capital Operating cost recovery or holdco-level fee
Leverage Rare Often used
Key risk Picking winners Integration, overpaying, culture, key-person

Implication: forcing B into a standard fund vehicle with a fixed 10-year life will either rush exits (destroying the compounding) or leave the LPs unclear on what they own. Options: 1. Separate vehicle / SPV for B, with its own term, fee and carry. 2. Continuation-fund style structure to move mature assets into a longer-hold vehicle with LP choice (roll or cash out). 3. Keep B as an option, not a promise: A produces the assets; B is only activated when ≥3 compatible companies exist.

Pre-conditions before B makes sense

  • At least 3 businesses with shared customer type or tech stack (otherwise "synergies" are theoretical).
  • A named platform operator (not the investment team part-time).
  • A clear standard integration playbook proven on the first two deals.
  • An agreed rule on what shared services are mandatory vs. optional (autonomy vs. control is the usual failure point).

Multiple arbitrage — be careful

The arbitrage is real only if the combined entity is actually more valuable per dollar (scale, diversification, predictable cash flows) and a buyer pays for it. Test it: who is the exit buyer for a $10m+ revenue platform of regional SaaS? If the answer is "a strategic or larger PE firm, at 1–2 turns higher", name three.

04Sub-strategy C — Service business buyout + AI modernization

What makes it attractive

  • Large, fragmented markets with owner-operators nearing retirement (succession-driven sellers).
  • Often low software maturity, so even moderate automation can move margins meaningfully.
  • Sticky customer relationships and regulatory moats (legal, insurance, accounting) can protect against pure-software disruption.

What is genuinely hard

  • The AI thesis is not a moat by itself. Tools are available to everyone; durable advantage comes from proprietary data, workflow integration, and distribution. Be specific about which of those you'd own.
  • Regulated professions have ownership rules. Law firms, accountancies and insurance intermediaries often have restrictions on who may own them (varies by country; some jurisdictions allow alternative business structures, others do not). Verify per jurisdiction before spending time on a target.
  • People are the product. Fee-earners/advisers leave if they feel replaced. Plan retention and incentive design.
  • Margin gains may be competed away — if automation lowers cost for the whole sector, prices follow. Advantage is in being early and capturing the savings in margin before price adjusts.
  • Different skills. Operational turnaround of a services business is not the same muscle as SaaS finance fix-ups. Requires different operators.

Suggested framing: test before building

Treat C as a pilot: one deal, a hard budget, explicit kill criteria (e.g. "if automation of workflow X doesn't give ≥X pts of margin within 12 months, we stop expanding"). Don't build a thesis document that implies a repeatable platform until one asset proves the playbook.

Market context

You list Constellation Software, Corgi, Lawhive, Dwelly and others. Worth building a short comparison table: owned vs. partnered, vertical, how they source, how they integrate, funding model. (A good next step — happy to research it, with source links.)

05Cross-cutting mechanics

Sourcing

  • Founder-side across all three. Build a pipeline list from: regional ecosystems, accelerator alumni, VC portfolios with stalled follow-on rounds, public signals (hiring freezes, leadership departures, dormant funding).
  • VCs as pipeline — frame as helping them with a clean exit for a portfolio company they would otherwise mark down. Relationship-driven; one clean transaction creates referral flow.

Valuation & structure

  • Prefer conservative revenue multiples with a gross-margin lens and cross-check with a standalone DCF.
  • Use earn-outs and rollover rather than paying for "potential".
  • Where founders are de facto in control (Lexar/smartocto-style), deals can be faster and cheaper — but also confirm informal side agreements, IP ownership and key-customer contracts.

AI as an operational lever

  • At company level: automated QA/testing, support deflection, leaner engineering, finance automation. Quantify per company: "hours saved × loaded cost".
  • At platform level (B): shared tooling, central data layer. Caution: don't centralize before the companies are stable.
  • Avoid "AI-washing": every claimed AI benefit should be a line in the value-creation plan with an owner and a metric.

Economics: the fee question

A bucket-specific management fee is defensible if it is clearly tied to operating resources actually deployed (CFO bench, operating partners, shared services), and not a general uplift on a venture fund fee. LPs will ask: - Is the fee on invested capital, NAV, or committed capital? - Are operating-partner costs charged to portfolio companies in addition (double dipping)? - How do carry and fee interact if the bucket has a different return profile? Recommended: a transparent cost-recovery model (fee ≈ budgeted operating team cost) plus carry, written into the LPA/side letter.

06Illustrative returns for sub-strategy A

Purely illustrative; assumes 100% ownership, no debt, ~$5m ARR at entry, entry at 2.0× ARR (EV $10m), plus ~$1m for turnaround costs ⇒ $11m total invested, 5-year hold. No fees or carry.

Scenario ARR CAGR ARR at exit Exit multiple Exit value MOIC
Downside 10% $8.1m 2.0× $16.1m ~1.5×
Base 25% $15.3m 3.5× $53.4m ~4.9×
Upside 35% $22.4m 5.0× $112m ~10×

What you need to believe for the base case: growth re-accelerates from the current rate to 25% sustained for five years and the exit multiple is 75% higher than entry. Stress-test by cutting either assumption — e.g. 20% CAGR and 3.0× exit gives ~3.4×; that is still attractive, which is the point of buying cheaply. The key sensitivity is entry multiple and growth durability, not the exit.

A simple model template (inputs: ARR, growth, margin, entry/exit multiples, hold, turnaround cost, ownership, rollover) would make this reusable per deal — a good next deliverable.

07Diligence checklist (A, adaptable to C)

Commercial: ARR bridge, cohort retention, NRR/GRR, pricing power, customer concentration (top-10 share), pipeline quality. Product/tech: tech debt, security posture, dependency on a single engineer, hosting costs, roadmap realism. Financial: gross margin by revenue type (subscription vs. services), capitalised development costs, deferred revenue, working capital, tax exposures, cash runway. Legal/cap table: full cap table incl. options/SAFEs/convertibles, preference terms, drag/tag, founder vesting, IP assignment, change-of-control clauses in key contracts. People: key-person map, compensation vs. market, retention packages, culture. Founder: honest conversation on motivation, post-deal role, and the plan if they leave.

09Suggested sequencing

  1. Now: define A's investment criteria precisely (size, growth, margin, geography, ownership %, ticket) and build a pipeline of 20–30 names.
  2. First 2–3 deals: execute A, document the playbook, measure value creation vs. plan.
  3. Decision gate: if ≥3 compatible companies and a named platform operator exist, design the vehicle for B.
  4. In parallel, low-cost: research C — one-page market map, regulatory review, shortlist of 1–2 candidate sectors; no capital committed until criteria are written.

10Open questions for discussion

  1. Ticket size and ownership target for A — is the deal flow fund-sized, or are many of these too small?
  2. Is the fund's current LPA flexible on control stakes, leverage, and fees for this bucket?
  3. Who are the operators (CFO bench, platform lead)? Internal hires, operating partners, or an external network?
  4. For B: what is the intended exit — sale of the platform, IPO, or indefinite hold?
  5. For C: which one or two sectors, and in which jurisdictions, are even allowed to be owned by a non-professional investor?
  6. How do we define "success" for the bucket: MOIC target, DPI timing, or platform value?
  7. What does the firm do better than a traditional lower-mid-market PE firm that targets the same assets?
